Building the Bridge: What We Heard at the 2026 California AgTech Alliance Convening

California does not lack promising ideas, agricultural expertise or technical talent. The challenge is connecting them. Here is what we heard at the 2026 Annual Convening.

By the California AgTech Alliance
Published October 19, 2026

Tags

NetworkStatewide

At one table, a pile of colorful building blocks became a bridge. Its pieces represented people, capital, infrastructure and agricultural technology: the elements participants believed must connect if innovation is to create lasting value across California.

“We talked about building the bridge between people, capital, infrastructure, and agriculture for prosperity, resilience, and equity across California,” one participant explained while presenting the table’s model.

That image captured the central idea of the 2026 California AgTech Alliance Annual Convening. California does not lack promising ideas, agricultural expertise or technical talent. The challenge is connecting them and ensuring that innovation begins with real needs, can be tested under real conditions and has a practical path to adoption.

Start with the people closest to the work

The event began with a panel discussion featuring members of the Alliance’s Industry Advisory Board, who brought the realities of agricultural technology adoption into focus. Panelists spoke about reliability, service, cost per acre, learning time and the narrow biological windows within which growers must make decisions.

Sal Parra, director of farming and operations at Bowles Farming Company, captured that urgency plainly: “If you come to me a week later, it’s too late. And if it took me a week to learn your system, then the opportunity’s gone. We’re working with living organisms, so there’s pests that have to be managed at a certain time.”

The conversation also emphasized trust. Growers want evidence from comparable operations, responsive support when something goes wrong and technologies that fit the rhythms of agricultural production. Adoption is not simply a matter of demonstrating that a tool works. It must work at the right moment, under the right conditions, for the people expected to use it.

Panelists also emphasized that technology should be developed with farmworkers, whose daily experience gives them deep knowledge of crops, equipment and production systems. As Kevin Boyle put it: “Farmers and workers have been integrating new tools for millennia and having to adapt for safety reasons, for productivity reasons, for performance reasons. … Is it going to be done to me? Is it going to be done for me? Or is it going to be done with me?”

Build pathways, not just products

A strong agricultural innovation ecosystem requires more than individual technologies. It also needs education, business assistance, opportunities for field testing and clear pathways through which people and ideas can move from classrooms and laboratories into farms, careers and markets.

The Alliance Project Showcase illustrated several parts of that pathway.

The Drones Uplifting California Communities initiative and its Train-the-Trainer work help educators develop local drone training programs. The effort combines technical instruction, curriculum and continuing support so educators can prepare a diverse workforce to use drones for mapping, monitoring and other practical applications.

GO-Biz and CalExport represent another part of the pathway: helping California businesses understand available resources, navigate government services and pursue opportunities in new markets.

The Farm Robotics Challenge begins earlier in the innovation process. Student teams work with a grower, rancher or land manager to identify an authentic agricultural problem before developing a possible solution.

“We’re not prescribing the problem that the students need to go and solve,” explained Kelly Scott of the Farm Robotics Challenge. “They have to have that communication with their ag partner, determine what really is happening on farm that needs a solution.” That approach makes listening to agricultural partners part of the engineering process rather than something that happens after a technology has already been designed.

The Future Food initiative at UC Merced added a research and testing perspective. Its envisioned network would connect complementary test sites, allowing technologies to be evaluated in appropriate agricultural settings before growers are asked to take on the risk of an unproven system.

Together, the programs demonstrated that progress depends on coordinated handoffs: from education to research, from research to field testing and from validation to the marketplace.

Make technology answer a real decision

Technical capability alone does not make a tool useful. Agricultural technology creates value when it helps someone make a better decision, complete a task more effectively or solve a problem that matters under real operating conditions. That requires trustworthy data, field validation, trained users and accessible support.

The Innovation in Practice presentations showed how companies are approaching those questions.

Innoptech described its work using drone and operations data to support decisions such as harvest forecasting, while emphasizing that growers should maintain control of their data.

AgTools widened the view beyond the field, demonstrating how information about market prices, volumes, weather, freight and other conditions can help producers and buyers interpret what is happening across a supply chain.

Cordon Technologies presented a precision-spraying approach designed to mix products on demand and record applications. Its presentation also underscored the importance of California field validation and connections with growers who can help assess the technology under working conditions.

Across the three presentations, the recurring lesson was clear: a promising technology also needs a practical operating model. Data quality, regulatory awareness, local service, workforce preparation and evidence from the field are all part of the product.

Connect statewide ambition to regional strength

California agriculture is too varied for one hub, test site or program model to serve every region equally. Crops, climates, institutions, infrastructure and workforce needs differ across the state. A statewide network becomes valuable when it strengthens regional capacity while connecting communities with complementary resources.

The regional innovation panel showed what that approach can look like.

Priscilla Koepke of F3 Innovate described its role in the Central Valley: “Our job as the neutral nonprofit to manage and build this innovation ecosystem is really to bring those partners together, but to be that front door to growers as a trusted partner, to understand what their problems are, what their needs are.”

Reservoir Farms discussed a network of sites where emerging technologies can encounter real crops, production systems and agricultural conditions.

Escondido AgX described its developing Southern California hub as connective tissue among schools, colleges, farms, businesses and engineering resources.

These regional efforts are not identical, because the communities they serve are not identical. That is a strength. A connected statewide network can help an innovator find the right crop, climate, expertise, workforce program or testing environment without asking every region to reproduce the same ecosystem.

Design for the full range of California farms

For innovation to strengthen California agriculture, it must work for farms with very different crops, acreage, budgets, labor structures and access to infrastructure. Purchase price is only one measure of fit. Learning time, versatility, repairability, reliability and the ability to test equipment before making an investment can matter just as much.

The small-farms discussion with the Community Alliance with Family Farmers and UC Cooperative Extension made those considerations tangible.

A diversified farm may not be able to justify equipment designed for only one crop. A farmer leasing land may hesitate to invest in permanent infrastructure. Even an inexpensive tool can become costly if it is unreliable, difficult to repair or takes too long to learn.

“With a small farm, the compounding factors of how much time it’s going to take that farm to learn how to do it, if there’s ongoing maintenance, if they need to learn how to fix something, if there’s any bugs that come up along the way or any issues with the equipment, that is even more important to a farmer. … I don’t know too many small farms who have an open week to learn something new. So that’s a huge factor for them,” said Elizabeth Vaughan of the Community Alliance with Family Farmers, who also farms five acres in San Diego County.

Panelists identified equipment-sharing and tool-lending programs as practical ways for farmers to evaluate technologies under their own conditions. They also called for farmers to participate throughout the innovation process, from defining the problem to testing prototypes and evaluating results. Farmers should also be recognized and compensated for the expertise they contribute, they said.

The session reinforced an essential principle: farmers are not simply customers at the end of an innovation pipeline. They are designers, evaluators and inventors within it.

Make impact visible

Connections become more useful when participants can see how their work relates to a larger system. The convening’s interactive building-block activity invited attendees to translate their hopes for the Alliance’s next five years into physical models.

Participants explored how the Alliance might benefit their work, colleagues and communities. Their models surfaced aspirations involving stronger networks, accessible testing, workforce opportunity and better connections among people, capital, infrastructure and agriculture.

The exercise did not produce a formal set of commitments. Instead, it made different understandings of impact visible and gave participants a concrete way to discuss what a successful innovation ecosystem could look like. Facilitators explained that the input would help inform the Alliance’s approach to evaluation and impact tracking.

Turn shared priorities into a useful roadmap

A roadmap is most valuable when the people expected to use it can recognize their needs and opportunities within it. The Alliance’s Research and Innovation Roadmap is therefore being developed as an ongoing process informed by growers, industry advisers, researchers, regional organizations and other partners.

During the roadmap workshop, participants considered how the emerging roadmap could be useful to their organizations, communities and regions. The discussion built on months of conversations among the Industry Advisory Board and Alliance partners.

The workshop was a step in continuing that development. It was not the announcement of a finalized roadmap or a claim that every priority had been settled. Its purpose was to gather additional perspectives and help shape a resource capable of guiding future collaboration.

Finance the journey from evidence to adoption

Agricultural innovation often faces a difficult middle stage. A concept may work, but it still needs repeated field evidence, customer learning, regulatory navigation, manufacturing capacity and time to become a durable business. Funding must reflect that full journey rather than treating a successful prototype as the finish line.

The investor panel brought together perspectives from SOSV, the Foundation for Food and Agriculture Research and GO-Biz.

The discussion returned repeatedly to credible field data, stronger handoffs between research and commercialization, and combinations of grants, equity, debt and public support suited to different stages of development.

Rather than presenting one funding formula, the panelists described a connected system: regional incubators and test sites, public and philanthropic support for early evidence, private capital for scalable businesses, and trusted partners who can help innovators navigate the available options.

They also challenged entrepreneurs and researchers to consider grower economics and the route to market from the beginning. Adoption cannot be left until the final stage of the development process.

From connection to action

The convening did not resolve every challenge, nor was it designed to. Instead, it made the necessary connections more visible: between farm knowledge and technical design, students and agricultural careers, startups and trial sites, regions and statewide resources, and evidence and investment.

Sarah Masterson closed the day with an invitation: “My ask, and the opportunity for all of you, for all of us, is to turn connection into action. … Take the next step with those connections. Think about what referral you can make or what introduction you can make.”

That action might be continuing a conversation, making an introduction, sharing a farm problem, offering a place to test an idea or bringing a promising opportunity to the Alliance when the next step is unclear.

That is how a bridge built in a meeting room can become lasting infrastructure for California agriculture.
